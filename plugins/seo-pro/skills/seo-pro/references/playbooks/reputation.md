# Reputation and digital authority playbook (R1–R14)

Digital PR, brand mentions, reviews and editorially earned links. It expands core practices 85–101, 104, 153, 154 and 157, Bing B16, AI A11, and the review practices in `../verticals/local.md` (L9–L11). Read `README.md` in this folder first.

Research snapshot: **18 October 2026**. Platform and consumer rules change and depend on jurisdiction; anything involving payment, affiliate links, influencers, health/finance or personal data needs editorial, commercial and legal review.

**The thesis.** Digital authority isn't a single metric you can buy or switch on with a campaign. It builds up when an organisation shows value, keeps its promises, publishes reliable information, looks after its customers and earns independent references. Links, mentions and reviews have **different mechanisms and different levels of evidence**: measure them separately.

Format: **Why** · **How** · **Avoid** · **Verify**.

## R1. Keep the concepts apart — High
- **Reputation:** perceptions and experiences customers, professionals, media, communities and platforms associate with an organisation, product or person.
- **Editorial or topical authority:** demonstrable competence in a field — experience, evidence, continuity, usefulness and independent recognition.
- **External link:** helps discovery, and Google documents that link analysis systems and PageRank are part of Search. **Established.**
- **Brand mention:** a textual or audiovisual reference, with or without a link. It can build awareness, coverage, trust or discovery; a direct ranking effect isn't confirmed. **Hypothesis.**
- **Review:** a person's opinion about an experience, on your platform or a third party's.
- **Vendor authority score** (Moz DA, Semrush Authority Score, Ahrefs DR, "toxicity"): a proprietary estimate over the vendor's own index — useful to triage, **not** a Google or Bing metric and never a contractual KPI.

## Digital PR with editorial judgement

### R2. Why would anyone independent cover it? — Critical
- **How:** before any outreach, answer: what does it add that doesn't exist or isn't easy to get? who does it help, what question does it answer? what evidence, data, access, expert source or original experience does it offer? why is it timely now — is the news real or manufactured? who can verify the claims and data? what conflicts or commercial relationships must be declared? what would make a journalist, association, specialist or community share it even with no SEO? If the main answer is "because our page needs authority", there's no editorial proposal yet.

### R3. Assets worth coverage — High
- **How:** original research with published method, limits and reusable data; a free tool, calculator, map, database or resource that solves a task (154); original documentation, expert analysis, technical guides, field data or product transparency; trend analysis with sample, dates and definitions; open technical contributions, standards or community projects; local knowledge, archives, interviews or community service; verifiable innovation or policy with sector impact; spokespeople with demonstrable expertise and fast answers (89, 90, 96).
- **Avoid:** novelty passed off as news; promotional content dressed as independent research; correlation presented as causation.

### R4. Outreach process — High
1. Research the audience and the author: beat, format, region, language and recent work.
2. Personalise: one idea per message; why it fits their work and what they can verify.
3. Offer evidence and access: method, spokesperson, data, screenshots, samples, documents and dates.
4. Facilitate, don't dictate: give the correct canonical URL and data; never impose anchor text, `follow`, headline or conclusion.
5. Declare relationships: payment, product sent, access, sponsorship, affiliation or professional ties.
6. Respect preferences and follow-up: keep a contact list, no pressure or repeated chasing, honour do-not-contact requests.
7. Log the result: coverage, link/mention, context, traffic, message pickup, leads and editorial lessons.
- The pitch must stand up without a link, and the coverage must make sense even if the brand isn't named in the first paragraph.

### R5. Sponsorships, collaborations and paid content — Critical
- **How:** sponsoring a publication, event, newsletter, podcast or creator can be a valid investment for audience, sales or reputation. Make the relationship clear to the public and qualify paid links with `rel="sponsored"` (`nofollow` is still accepted by Google for this; 100, 101).
- **Avoid:** disguising advertising as independent recommendation; requiring a ranking-passing link as a condition of payment. "Editorial packages with high DR and guaranteed dofollow" are link buying and link-spam risk, not earned PR.

## Mentions, entity and brand

### R6. Value an unlinked mention for what it does — Medium · *Hypothesis as a ranking signal*
- **How:** a mention can make an audience aware of a brand, person or product; prompt a direct visit or a later brand search; help customers, journalists, partners and specialists find you; open a door to future linked references; add public context. Its value depends on who publishes it, who it reaches, context, accuracy, tone, permanence and actionability. Measure it as exposure, brand demand and indirect referral (R13); offer the exact URL politely if a link would help readers (91).
- **Avoid:** claiming a mention passes PageRank like a link; buying "inauthentic brand mentions" — Google's spam policies now explicitly cover manipulation aimed at its AI features (A11).

### R7. Consistent, verifiable identity — High
- **How:** consistent facts across the site, profiles, documents and public records: name, legal entities, products, address, phone, areas, people, biographies, certifications and dates (38, 104, 106). On the site, show who publishes and who answers for each claim; contact and support routes; editorial/commercial, affiliate, privacy, returns, warranty and methodology policies as relevant; verifiable references and primary sources; correction history where precision matters (157). Consistency helps people verify you're the same organisation; it doesn't turn every external listing into a ranking signal.

### R8. Brand listening and response — Medium
- **How:** listen for brand terms and variants, products, spokespeople, look-alike domains and support queries (91). Classify each finding by accuracy, tone, reach, customer relevance, privacy/security risk, amplification potential and required response. Escalate threats, personal data, impersonation or serious allegations through the proper channels.
- **Avoid:** turning listening into surveillance of individuals or doxxing critics; collecting more than the information needed and legitimately available.

## Earned links

### R9. Earn links; never buy or rent your reputation — Critical · *Established (Google link spam and site reputation abuse policies)*
- **What makes a link valuable to a reader:** context (part of a coherent explanation), audience (readers relate to the destination), motive (an editorial, factual or service reason), destination (keeps its promise and is accessible), independence (no hidden consideration) and transparency (commercial ties labelled and paid links qualified). Relevance to the reader beats a domain-authority number.
- **Legitimate methods:** journalist relationships built on timely information and access; publishing research, methods and data others can cite; references to technical docs, resources, datasets and primary sources; genuine professional collaboration without systematic link swaps; respectful broken-link recovery with a truly equivalent substitute (87, 94); correcting factual mentions with verifiable data, offering — not demanding — the exact URL; disclosed sponsorships with `sponsored`; association or sector directories where you're a legitimate member and the listing serves users (98); expert editorial contributions with authorship and conflicts declared.
- **Avoid:** buying "dofollow" links, backlink packages, scaled guest posts with exact-match anchors or private blog networks; repeated link exchanges, partner rings or resource pages built to link to each other (95); sending products in exchange for a mandatory review or link; mass press releases with optimised anchors (96); links in irrelevant comments, profiles, forums or directories; expired domains, site networks or scaled content to host backlinks; faked authorship, awards, testimonials or independence of a comparison. Google defines link spam as creating links primarily to manipulate rankings. **Site reputation abuse** — hosting third-party pages mainly to exploit the host's ranking signals — is also spam, even when the host reviews part of it; don't rent out sections of a trusted site, and don't buy space on one (`../verticals/publishers.md` P17, `governance.md` G13). Bing names link schemes and "artificial social promotion" (B16).

### R10. Suspicious links: investigate before disavowing — High · *Established (Google disavow guidance)*
- **How:** Google says most sites don't need the disavow tool; it's for advanced cases, mainly artificial links created by you or your agency that can't be removed, after a manual action or a significant history of schemes (153). Process: (1) check Manual actions in Search Console; (2) identify who created the links and whether they were a paid/manipulative campaign; (3) prioritise documented removal requests where feasible; (4) disavow only if Google's conditions are met and the person applying it understands the impact; (5) keep inventory, reason, evidence, approval and file version.
- **Avoid:** acting because a crawler labels many links "toxic" or the profile grows fast; retaliation against third-party negative links.

## Reviews

### R11. An ethical, sustainable review programme — Critical
- **How:** ask real customers for feedback neutrally, at a moment that fits the experience, through an appropriate channel. On Google Business Profile you can remind customers with a link or QR (L9), but you can't offer a benefit, require a rating or text, pressure in the store, or ask only those who seem satisfied. Design: send the invitation broadly and consistently to all eligible customers; allow public **or** private feedback without one blocking the other; no satisfaction survey that routes only happy customers to Google (**review gating**); no requested keywords, employee names or set phrases; no compensation for reviews on platforms that forbid it; keep paid research panels separate from public reviews and label relationships; limit repeated requests and allow opt-out; measure request rate, response, themes, resolution and satisfaction, not only the star average.
- **Google Maps rules:** prohibited — reviews not reflecting a real experience, reviews paid in cash or kind, asking for reviews/removals in exchange for benefits, discouraging negative feedback, soliciting only positive reviews, pressuring customers, requesting specific content. Violations can lead to review removal and Business Profile restrictions, including temporary blocks on new reviews or public notices that fake reviews were removed.
- **EU and Spain (not legal advice):** consumer law reinforced by Directive 2019/2161 requires transparency on whether and how traders check reviews come from people who used or bought the product, and prohibits fake reviews or endorsements and misleading manipulation; obligations depend on your role (trader, platform, review provider), market and national law (Spain: consolidated Real Decreto Legislativo 1/2007). On your own site, publish how reviews are collected, ordered, verified and moderated; keep critical reviews under transparent rules; disclose incentives; never claim "verified" without a process. The US FTC rule (October 2024) applies in its jurisdiction.

### R12. Responding to reviews and review markup — High
- **Responding to negative reviews:** check whether it's a real experience, a mix-up, a service failure or policy-violating content; reply calmly without revealing personal or order data; acknowledge the experience and explain the next step; offer a private channel to resolve details, never buying removal or edits; fix the operational cause and confirm follow-up; report only reviews that actually breach policy; document patterns by product, location, delivery, support and expectation. No defensive, threatening, identical-template or promotional replies.
- **Markup:** `Review`/`AggregateRating` only when it matches the page, entity type and visible content. Self-serving reviews of an `Organization` or `LocalBusiness` about itself aren't eligible for review snippets (85, L15). Don't copy Google Maps stars onto your site as if they were your own reviews or invent ratings to trigger rich results.

## R13. Measure PR, mentions, links and reviews — High

| Level | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Activity | What did the team do? | Personalised pitches, replies, interviews, resources published |
| Output | What coverage was earned? | Stories, mentions, links, context, country, estimated audience |
| Outcome | What did the audience do? | Referral traffic, brand searches, qualified visits, sign-ups, contacts |
| Impact | What business or reputational result changed? | Accepted leads, sales, retention, measured trust, fewer incidents |
| Observed SEO | What changed in organic search? | Brand and non-brand clicks/impressions, cited pages, relevant links |
| Causal SEO | What change was additional? | Lift vs control or a counterfactual design with published assumptions |

- **Useful metrics:** relevant editorial coverage, story accuracy and spokesperson presence; new links that are real, live, contextual and relevant (URL, destination, date); unlinked mentions kept separate from backlinks; referral sessions with landing and business outcome (consistent UTM taxonomy where it doesn't alter the editorial URL); brand searches and direct navigation as trends, not isolated proof (X9); reviews by volume, distribution, age, theme, location and resolution, plus the average; calls, visits, bookings, conversions and qualified customers; share of voice only with stable coding; manual review of quality, credibility and diversity of publishers.
- **Attribution limits:** coverage creates exposure that never shows as referral (people remember, search later, switch device, convert offline); a referral visit doesn't prove coverage caused the conversion. For causal claims use comparable markets, pages, business lines or windows with intervention/control; log concurrent media, campaigns, launches, seasonality and product changes; report interval, sample, assumptions and spillovers (a national story contaminates controls) — `measurement.md` X11–X12. Without identification, describe timing and supporting evidence, not "ROI generated".

## R14. Reputation incident protocol — High
- **Before:** owners for support, PR, legal/privacy, product and leadership; a register of recurring issues and severity; response channels, target times and approval rules; current contact, policies, warranties and organisation data; a correction and escalation mechanism; evidence and logs of impersonation, harassment or coordinated reviews kept without publishing private data.
- **During:** confirm facts and scope before responding publicly; identify harm to customers and prioritise fixing it; say what's known, what's being investigated and when the next update comes; never delete legitimate criticism or threaten people who share an experience; correct objectively wrong pages, profiles or processes; for illegal content, impersonation or personal data use the proper channels with advice — never promise a platform will remove it; log actions and measure resolution, not only mention volume.
- **After:** publish corrections where appropriate, fix the root cause, respond to affected people, and review whether support, suppliers, the PR agency or moderation allowed the problem. A reputation campaign must never cover up a real product defect.

## Myths

| Myth / tactic | Assessment | Legitimate alternative |
|---|---|---|
| "An unlinked mention passes the same PageRank" | No public rule supports it | Measure exposure, brand demand, indirect referral, relationship |
| "We need dofollow links" | Imposing attributes or anchors makes it manipulation | Useful references; qualify paid placements |
| "More links always means more authority" | Volume can include spam, duplicates and irrelevance | Independent, useful references; assess context and risk |
| "DA is a ranking factor" | A vendor score, not a Google metric | An auxiliary indicator inside its own tool |
| "Ask only happy customers for reviews" | Review gating; Google forbids selective positive requests | Neutral, broad requests to eligible customers |
| "A discount for five stars is fine if declared" | Google Maps forbids it; legality varies | No benefits conditioned on public reviews |
| "We can delete all negative reviews" | Genuine opinions don't breach policy for being negative | Reply, resolve, report only real violations |
| "Review schema switches on stars" | No guarantee; self-hosted reviews may be ineligible | Mark up only visible, eligible, useful content |
| "Disavow every toxic link" | Google says most sites don't need disavow | Investigate origin, manual action and own involvement first |
| "A press release gives safe backlinks" | Paid/mass distribution with optimised anchors can be link spam | Treat it as communication; disclose and qualify paid links |

## 90-day sequence

- **Days 1–15 — base and policy:** inventory profiles, review platforms, backlinks, mentions and spokespeople; review the policy on link building, gifts, sponsorships, affiliates, reviews and moderation; baseline brand queries, referrals, reviews, links and business results; fix outdated public facts and contacts.
- **Days 16–35 — experience and listening:** map the frictions behind negative reviews and fix one priority operational cause; response and escalation guides and a neutral request process; brand listening with tags for coverage, sentiment and risk; identify 2–3 assets with verifiable editorial value.
- **Days 36–60 — relationships and assets:** interview journalists, communities, associations and experts about information gaps; publish one original resource with method, transparency and an owner; personalised outreach with no link, anchor or follow conditions; document sponsorships with disclosure and `rel="sponsored"`.
- **Days 61–90 — analysis and improvement:** review coverage, context, references and quality traffic; analyse brand searches, CRM results and mentions by date without automatic causal claims; share recurring review themes by product/location with operations; audit new links and review specially only where there's evidence of a scheme or a manual action; replicate initiatives by audience and business value, not link counts.

## Executive checklist

**PR and links**
- [ ] The story brings data, knowledge, access or a verifiable resource.
- [ ] The pitch targets a relevant audience/outlet and respects preferences.
- [ ] No required link, anchor, follow attribute or editorial conclusion.
- [ ] Payments, gifted products, affiliate ties and conflicts disclosed.
- [ ] Paid placements qualified per search engine policy.
- [ ] Reports separate mentions, links, referral traffic and results.

**Reviews**
- [ ] Requests are neutral and sent to eligible customers, not only satisfied ones.
- [ ] No conditioned incentives, gating, pressure or requested text.
- [ ] Professional replies, privacy protected, a resolution route.
- [ ] Reviews shown and verified with transparent criteria.
- [ ] Markup reflects visible content and eligibility was checked.

**Authority and reputation**
- [ ] Credentials and facts backed by evidence.
- [ ] Impersonation, errors, critical mentions and service themes monitored.
- [ ] DA/DR and toxicity treated as tool estimates.
- [ ] No pre-emptive disavows without diagnosis.
- [ ] Each campaign has an audience/business goal, baseline and evaluation method.
